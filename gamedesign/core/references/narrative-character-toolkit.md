# Narrative — Character Design Toolkit

Detailed character-design catalog extracted from `narrative.md` (load on demand
when authoring a protagonist, antagonist, or supporting cast). The main rule
keeps the conceptual hook; this file holds the full toolkit. Sources: Bryant &
Giglio, *Slay the Dragon*; Heussner et al., *The Game Narrative Toolbox*; Jesse
Schell, *The Art of Game Design*, Ch 17–20.

## Playable Characters

A character sheet pairs **fiction** (role, voice, arc, relationships) with a
**gameplay function** — vendor, tutorial guide, gate-keeping antagonist, a companion
whose mechanics express their personality. Rich lore with no function is content
debt; a central system with no voice reads as a system, not a person. Both halves
or cut one.

- **Write from the arc backwards.** Decide the protagonist's emotional end-state
  first, then engineer the character and plot to force that change (Michael
  Corleone, Kratos are built from their destination backwards).
- **Maximize emotional distance.** Choose the protagonist with the most ground to
  travel between who they start as and who they become. Power growth (new weapons,
  levels) is a *gameplay* arc — demand an *emotional* arc too.
- **Want vs need.** *Want* is the conscious goal; *need* is the deeper thing they
  actually require. Put them at war — that internal conflict beneath the external
  one is the craft. *The Last of Us*: Joel *wants* to be left alone, *needs* to be a
  parent again (the same technique as *Finding Nemo*'s Marlin).
- **Conflict is the essence of drama.** External conflict (enemies, puzzles,
  hazards) is easy; internal conflict is the hard, interesting part.
- **Superman vs Batman.** The compelling hero is flawed, vulnerable, self-made, and
  *active* (Batman), not flawless, powerful, and passive (Superman). The
  **ordinary-person-in-extraordinary-circumstances** formula breeds empathy ("what
  if this happened to me?" → "this *is* happening to me").
- **Backstory as "the ghost."** A past trauma (almost always loss) that haunts the
  protagonist and explains their flaws and drives their decisions. Reveal it early,
  via flashback, or merely hint at it — but make it *motivate*, not decorate.
- **Reduce motivation to a primal emotion** — love of family, survival, revenge.
  Primal motives let players connect viscerally. Always ask *why*.
- **Gameplay as method acting**: let the player understand the character's motives
  and perform their actions; avatar *creation* is casting, not character writing.

## Antagonists & Supporting Cast

"A story is only as good as its villain." Obstacles are personified by antagonists;
a game without obstacles is not a game.

- **"The bad guys think it's their game."** Build the villain with the same
  tools — want, need, conflict. The villain is the **hero of their own story**; from
  their POV the player is the invader (*Wreck-It Ralph* told from the "villain's"
  side; Andrew Ryan genuinely wanted to build a utopia).
- The **only hard requirement**: the villain's want and the hero's want are
  **mutually exclusive**. Villains need not be cartoonishly evil (Sam Gerard in
  *The Fugitive*).
- Antagonists can be **personal** (a named rival) or **impersonal** (the
  environment, a force of nature). Give bosses *personality* (*Metal Gear Solid*'s
  named bosses hold up for decades), not mute monsters to plow through.
- **Supporting NPCs** supply clues, backstory, direction, companionship, and comic
  relief — they build up and challenge the protagonist. But don't let an NPC outshine
  a hollow PC (you remember GLaDOS, not Chell) — push the PC to be as compelling as
  the supporting cast.

## Character Web & Character Tools

Game characters trend **emotional → physical, real → fantastic, complex →
simple** vs novel/film characters (Schell): players supply the inner life, so
game characters act more than they speak. That's the easy default — depth is
*hard* but possible. Schell's toolkit complements the want/need/ghost arc above
(don't re-derive those here):

- **The avatar** (Schell's Lens of the Avatar) — the playable character is the
  *gateway* the player projects into (we project onto anything we control — our
  car, a bowling ball). Two projection-friendly forms, often combined:
  - **Idealized form** — the hero the player *wishes to be* (warrior, mage, spy).
  - **Blank slate** (McCloud's iconic/"portrait" character) — fewer facial/identity
    details = easier self-projection; pair a simple iconic avatar with a *detailed*
    world for maximum pull (Mario; Spider-Man = ideal form + a mask that blanks him
    out). Note: **players don't want to *be themselves*** — face-import systems
    chronically fail because people play to be who they *want* to be.
- **Character traits** (Lens of Character Traits) — distil each character to a
  short list of essential, even contradictory traits, then make **every line and
  action express them** (elegance: pack >1 trait per beat). Flat exposition
  ("someone stole the crown!") becomes characterization when filtered through
  traits (impulsive/pious/hot-tempered/loyal). Traits should also shape *how* an
  animation reads (a sly jump, a dejected character who can only walk).
- **The interpersonal circumplex** (Lens of Status/relationships) — plot each
  character on two axes, **hostile↔friendly** and **submissive↔dominant**, relative
  to a chosen focal character. Look for *extreme* characters at the corners (they're
  the interesting ones), empty quadrants worth filling, and whether allies sit in
  the *same* or *different* quadrants. Map it for Luke, then for Vader, and compare.
- **The character web** (Schell's character web, Lens of the Character Web) — for
  *every* character, write what they feel about *every other* character and *why*
  (the Archie-comics example: Archie↔Veronica↔Betty↔Reggie↔Jughead, each pair
  asymmetric). **Relationships, not characters in isolation, create interest.** It's
  cheap and surfaces interactions you'd otherwise miss; hunt for missing links and
  for too-uniform relationships.
- **Status** (Keith Johnstone's *Impro*; Schell's Lens of Status) — every
  interaction is a hidden status negotiation; status is **what you *do*, not who you
  are**, and it's *relative* (Vader plays high to Leia, low to the Emperor). Low
  status: fidgeting, broken eye contact, self-touching, taking marginal space. High
  status: stillness (notably a still head while speaking), steady eye contact,
  claiming central space; can be signaled by slow-motion. **Status swings are
  inherently interesting** — characters jockeying to raise their own / lower the
  other's is what makes an argument gripping (*Munch's Oddysee*'s slave-vs-overlord
  dynamics). Give NPCs status-awareness and they instantly read more alive; ask how
  the *player* shows status too.
- **Character transformation** (Schell's Lens of Character Transformation) — strong
  stories *change* their characters; games default to static (a villain stays a
  villain) and bore. If you can't transform the PC, transform a companion or villain
  (*Fable*, KOTOR). Build a **transformation table**: characters down the side, story
  beats across the top, mark where each changes — track *every* character's arc, not
  just the plot.
- **Let characters surprise** (Fitzgerald's actor advice via Schell) — a reaction
  *opposite* to what the audience expects ("show her weak when they think her
  strong") seizes attention (an instance of the surprise lens; *Breaking Bad*).
- **The face & the voice** — the eyes are highest-value: dead eyes kill a character;
  alive eyes revive the whole figure (cheap to animate — brows + eye shape). Surface
  the face even when the avatar faces away (*Doom*'s HUD portrait tied face to
  health). **Record voice the right way round**: film records VO *first* then builds
  character; games record it *last*, starving performance — push actors earlier in
  the pipeline. There's "no such thing as bad voice acting" only as far as the
  *dialogue and pipeline* are good.
- **The uncanny valley** (Mori) — empathy rises with human-likeness, then crashes
  into revulsion just short of real (zombies, "CG humans" in motion). Realism-chasing
  game characters risk the fall; stylized/iconic characters (Pixar, *Wind Waker*)
  sit safely on the near slope.
