## Worlds & Transmedia

Stories and games happen in **worlds**, and the most valuable end product is
often not a story or a game but a *world* (Schell, via Henry Jenkins'
**transmedia worlds**). A world exists independently of any one medium; each
product — game, film, comic, toy, card game — is a **portal** into it. Kids
playing with *Star Wars* figures rarely re-enacted the film; they used the toys
as portals to invent their own stories in that *world*. Build the world to be
deeper and more enduring than any single game it ships in.

- **What makes a fictional world deep, real, and enduring:**
  - **Know your world like a god** (McKee) — imagine it as lived-in, with
    answers to its history and every character's motivation. If it isn't real to
    *you*, it won't be real to players; lazy worlds read as unimaginative.
  - **Internal consistency is fragile** — "a spoonful of wine in a barrel of
    sewage is still sewage." One contradiction can **"jump the shark"** and
    permanently break belief in the whole world; hold your world-rules and take
    them seriously (the pocket that holds a microwave must also hold the ironing
    board).
  - **A world larger than any one story** — enduring worlds are never built
    around a single plot; they leave room for **many stories**, including ones
    fans invent.
  - **Simplicity + transcendence** (Schell's Lens of Simplicity/Transcendence) —
    game worlds offer a world *simpler* than the real one **and** powers
    *greater* than the real one. This pairing explains the recurring settings:
    medieval (primitive tech + magic), future/post-apocalyptic (broken world +
    clarke-magic tech), war (rules suspended + godlike weapons), modern-crime
    (*GTA*: no laws = simpler + stronger), abstract (*Minecraft*: simpler than
    even most games + total control). Ask: how is my world simpler? what
    transcendent powers do I grant? does the combo feel wish-fulfilling, not
    contrived?
- **Properties of transmedia worlds:** they are **powerful** (a personal utopia
  fans want to visit — Lens of Fantasy: what fantasy does my world make real, who
  do players get to be, what would they do there?); **long-lived** (Superman 70+
  yrs, Bond, Star Trek — a strong world earns money for decades and passes from
  parents to children); and **evolving** (Sherlock's deerstalker + pipe and
  Santa's reindeer entered the canon via *later* portals — the collective audience,
  not the original author, decides what sticks).
- **What successful transmedia worlds share** — they (1) sprang from **one
  defining medium** (Holmes a story, Superman a comic, Pokémon a video game,
  Transformers a toy) yet each portal is **self-sufficient and welcoming** (never
  "you'll get it if you read the book" — that's why *Enter the Matrix* failed);
  (2) are **intuitive**; (3) trace to a **single creative vision / small tight
  team**, rarely a big committee; (4) **support many stories**; (5) **reward
  exploration**; (6) **fulfill a wish**. The "binocular effect": a richer portal
  (the Pokémon anime) re-colors the player's imagination of a thinner one (the
  Game Boy game) — keep portals *mutually consistent* or the world "turns to dust."
- **Franchise designers** are increasingly asked to author *new portals into
  existing worlds* (a hard craft) — or, most valuable, to invent a new enduring
  transmedia world from understanding the audience's hidden desires (Lens of the
  World: why is my world better than the real one? can it be entered many ways?
  does it hold many stories?).
- **The strangest thing** (Lens of the Strangest Thing) — one weird element can
  justify a weird mechanic and make the world special, but too many strange things
  overwhelm. Introduce the strangest thing carefully so it doesn't confuse (the
  "Hamster Cannons on Sale!" sign that primes the player before they meet the
  cannon). Also: **make the world *accessible*, not accurate** — Verne used a
  cannon, not a rocket, because it was more believable *to his audience*; what the
  player will believe and enjoy beats literal truth.
