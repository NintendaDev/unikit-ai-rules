# What Triggers Player Emotion — Audience-Facing Inventory (Sylvester)

Lookup catalog for `player-motivation.md`. Open this when you need a *what-to-evoke*
menu for a target audience — the recurring desires a design can reach to pull a given
audience in. It complements Quantic Foundry's *who* (in the main rule) with a *what*.

Sylvester (*Designing Games*, Ch 1) treats a game as an **experience engine**:
mechanics generate events, events shift **human values** (states that matter to
people — life/death, victory/defeat, friend/stranger/foe, wealth/poverty,
high/low status, together/alone, knowledge/ignorance, danger/safety…), and a
*shift* in a human value is what fires an emotion. The mechanism itself
(value-shift, anticipation = realization, misattribution) is framework-level theory —
see the frameworks rule. The list below overlaps QF/LeBlanc on several entries
(noted) while adding ones the spectra don't name (acquisition, beauty, environment,
spectacle, music, threat).

| Sylvester trigger | The player wants | QF / LeBlanc overlap | Cost / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Learning / insight** | the "aha" of mastering a hidden hierarchy; lessons that touch real human values (build, socialize, fight) hook hardest | Challenge, Discovery | needs a deep, non-obvious system to reveal over time; trivial games (tic-tac-toe) can't deliver it |
| **Challenge / competence** | the flow of a fair test, the rush of triumph, "try again" pull | Challenge, Strategy; Hard Fun | powerful but *optional* — not every game needs struggle (The Sims, Dear Esther) |
| **Character evolution (empathy)** | to project into a character revealed through conflict; loss/growth, "getting into others' heads" | Fantasy, Story | needs interlocking character/mechanic structure; can be pre-authored or emergent |
| **Social interaction** | besting/befriending *real people*; status, trust, reputation, bragging, gifting | Competition, Community; People Fun | the value must be a *real* social shift — beating a human ≠ beating the AI |
| **Acquisition** | the pleasure of getting richer — gold, loot, gear, XP; fake wealth still tickles the reward centre | Power, Completion | the engine of grind loops; thin on its own, easy to over-rely on |
| **Spectacle** | instant visual jolt (explosions, slow-mo) | — | cheap creatively, expensive to build; only works to *amplify* an earned emotion, never standalone |
| **Beauty** | pleasant sensation from form, color, grace | Sensation | not free; can clash with a dark/tense aesthetic and add interpretive noise |
| **Music / sound** | a constant subconscious emotional undercurrent | — (Sensation-adjacent) | the most under-credited trigger; overuse backfires |
| **Environment** | place-as-mood (savanna-preference, weather, season) | — | strong and varied; can carry a whole game's feeling (Metro 2033, Heavy Rain) |
| **Threat / disgust / sexual signals** | hard-wired reflex reactions | — | cheapest and most abused; blunted by overuse, and gratuitous use repels a broad audience |

Design reads:
- **Match the trigger to the audience.** A trigger is only motivating if it shifts
  a value *this* audience cares about — the same way a QF motivation only counts
  if the comp-set's players actually praise it. Acquisition-led loot loops suit a
  Power/Completion audience; an Immersion–Creativity audience indexes on
  environment, learning, and character.
- **Stack triggers for one feeling, don't fight them.** Several triggers aimed at
  the same emotion compound (action arcades pile music + risk + competition +
  adrenaline). Antagonistic pairings cancel (ruthless skill-competition kills
  relaxed social play — why friends agree to play "just for fun").
- **Spectacle, beauty, threat, and sexual signals are amplifiers, not engines.**
  They give a fast hit but decay with overuse and can crowd out deeper, quieter
  sources (learning, character, social). Treat a design that *leads* with them as
  a red flag, not a strength.
- **Mechanics-only vs story-added.** Bare mechanics reach tension, triumph,
  learning, and social rewards but barely reach humor, awe, immersion, or the
  empathy-driven spectrum; the story layer adds a second meaning-layer on top of
  the same mechanical emotion. The strongest designs fuse the two rather than
  privileging either.

> Source: Tynan Sylvester, *Designing Games* (O'Reilly, 2013), Ch 1.
